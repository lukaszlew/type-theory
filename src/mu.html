Classical logic has proofs that System F has not. One is a proof of Peirce’s law, ((X → Y) → X) → X. Read as terms, such proofs need a new form of term. Parigot’s λμ-calculus has one: a term that takes hold of the arguments it is applied to and puts them after named places inside itself. Limit 4 of {{limits}} said what a new form of term costs: the definitions under the theorem are read again from the start. This section reads {{terms}} and {{head}} again for the new terms. {{classical}} reads again what the soundness theorem needs, and finds a rule that the three conditions of {{typeset}} do not make true.

Definition · λμ-terms

There is a second supply of variables, the names, written α, β, γ.

A λμ-term is a variable x, an abstraction λx.t, an application t u, or a μ-abstraction μα.[β]t, where t and u are λμ-terms and α and β are names.

In μα.[β]t the part [β]t is called a named term: the term t, named β.

The μα binds the name α in [β]t, in the name in front as well if β is α.

A name is free in a term if it occurs in it, as the name of a named term, at a place that is not inside a μ that binds it.

A λμ-term is closed if no variable and no name is free in it.

For names α and α′, t[α/α′] is t with α put for every free α′, with the bound names of t chosen different from α.

Terms are taken up to the choice of bound variables and bound names, as in {{terms}}, and a substitution t[u/x] chooses the bound names of t, like its bound variables, so that no free name of u becomes bound. Every term of {{terms}} is a λμ-term, and for those “closed” means what it meant. Quoted: the calculus is Parigot’s, and the way of writing the terms that is given here, in which a μ is always followed by a name, is taken from the paper of van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro. The letter μ is Parigot’s and has nothing to do with the least fixed point μF of {{more}}; the word “name” is his too, and has nothing to do with a type expression being the name of a set in {{names}}.

Definition · Appending at a name

For a term t, a name α and a term u, t[α⇐u] is t with u appended at α: every named term [α]w inside t, with this α free in t, becomes [α](w′ u), where w′ is w treated in the same way. Nothing is done inside the copies of u.

Clause by clause, with the bound names of t chosen different from α, and the bound variables and bound names of t not free in u: x[α⇐u] = x; (λx.t)[α⇐u] = λx.t[α⇐u]; (t s)[α⇐u] = t[α⇐u] s[α⇐u]; (μγ.[β]t)[α⇐u] = μγ.[β]t[α⇐u] if β is not α; and (μγ.[α]t)[α⇐u] = μγ.[α](t[α⇐u] u).

The same is written for a named term: ([β]t)[α⇐u] is [β]t[α⇐u] if β is not α, and ([α]t)[α⇐u] is [α](t[α⇐u] u).

For a list v⃗ = v1,…,vn of terms, t[α⇐v⃗] appends them all, in order and in one pass: [α]w becomes [α](w′ v1…vn). For the empty list nothing changes.

If α is not free in the terms of a list, appending them one after another is appending the list: for two terms, t[α⇐v1][α⇐v2] = t[α⇐v1,v2], because the second pass then finds nothing to do inside the copies of v1, and for more terms in the same way. Without that condition the two can differ: for t = μγ.[α]x, v1 = μγ.[α]y and v2 = z, the first is μγ.[α](x (μγ.[α](y z)) z) and the second is μγ.[α](x (μγ.[α]y) z). The literature calls the operation structural substitution.

Definition · Steps of λμ-terms

There are two rules. The first is β, as in {{terms}}: (λx.t) u → t[u/x].

The second is called μ: (μα.[β]t) u → μα.([β]t)[α⇐u], with the bound name α chosen so that it is not free in u. Written out, the result is μα.[β]t[α⇐u] if β is not α, and μα.[α](t[α⇐u] u) if β is α.

A step applies one of the two rules to a subterm, anywhere inside a term. Runs, normal forms and SN are defined from steps as in {{terms}}.

After a μ step the μ-abstraction is still there, so it takes every argument it is given, one step for each. A μ-abstraction that is given no argument has no step of its own. A choice, and its price. Parigot’s calculus has more rules: one that renames, [β](μγ.[α]t) to [α]t with β put for γ; one that removes μα.[α]t in favour of t if α is not free in t; and in some presentations a mirror image of μ, in which a μ-abstraction in the place of an argument takes hold of the function. They are left out here, as η is left out in {{terms}}, and every statement about SN in this section and in {{classical}} is about runs made of β and μ steps. The remark “What is in the literature, and what was left out” in {{classical}} reports what is known with the other rules.

Example · Three runs

  • Arguments kept. (μα.[α]x) y z → (μα.[α](x y)) z, and then → μα.[α](x y z). Both arguments end up after the term named α, in order.
  • An argument thrown away. (μα.[γ]y) u → μα.[γ]y. The name α does not occur, so the argument is thrown away. So (μα.[γ]y) Ω has a step to a normal form and is not in SN, like (λx.y) Ω.
  • A loop through μ. Let m = μα.[α]δ. Then m m → μα.[α](δ m) → μα.[α](m m), and the last term contains m m again. So m m ∉ SN.

Definition · Head steps of λμ-terms

Every λμ-term has exactly one of five forms, with n ≥ 0: an abstraction λx.t; a μ-abstraction μα.[β]t; a variable applied to arguments, x u1…un; an abstraction applied to arguments, (λx.t) u u1…un; a μ-abstraction applied to arguments, (μα.[β]t) u u1…un.

The last two forms have a head step. For an abstraction it is the one of {{head}}. For a μ-abstraction it is

(μα.[β]t) u u1…un →h (μα.([β]t)[α⇐u]) u1…un

The argument of either head step is the term u, and the head step is safe if u ∈ SN.

Base is, as in {{typeset}}, the set of the terms x u1…un with every ui ∈ SN.

The first three forms have no head step. A head step of either sort survives one more argument, with the same argument, as in {{head}}. Base does not grow: a μ-abstraction applied to an argument always has a step, so no new form sits still in front of its arguments the way a variable does.

Lemma R, for λμ-termshere, reviewed

steps kept
If t → t′, then t[α⇐u] → t′[α⇐u] and t[u/x] → t′[u/x]. If u → u′, then t[α⇐u] reaches t[α⇐u′], and t[u/x] reaches t[u′/x], by zero or more steps.
names
Let t be a term and let α and α′ be names. If t → s, then t[α/α′] → s[α/α′]. So if t[α/α′] ∈ SN, then t ∈ SN.
R1 and R2
R1 (subterms) and R2 (variable applied) of {{head}} hold as stated there.
under μ
μα.[β]t ∈ SN if and only if t ∈ SN.
R3
Safe head step, of either sort. If s →h s′ is safe and s′ ∈ SN, then s ∈ SN. Written out for the new sort, with c short for the named term ([β]t)[α⇐u]: if (μα.c) u1…un ∈ SN and u ∈ SN, then (μα.[β]t) u u1…un ∈ SN.

The word “safe” matters for the new head step as for the old one, by the run an argument thrown away of the example “Three runs”; it matters only if the name α does not occur, as for β it matters only if x is not free, because otherwise the argument is a subterm of the term after the step. Quoted. R3 for both sorts of head step, and the “if” halves of R2 and of the item under μ, are stated without proof as one proposition in the paper of van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro, and David and Nour prove the like of R3 for another presentation of the calculus. The proofs here were written for this page by readers of those papers; the item names was added by the reader who reviewed them, and Battyányi and Nour state the like, in both directions and for more rules, as a lemma of their paper.

Proof

Steps kept. Four identities are used, each by induction on c, a term or a named term. Appending passes through a substitution: c[w/x][α⇐u] = c[α⇐u][w[α⇐u]/x], for x not free in u. Appending at two names can be done in either order: c[γ⇐w][α⇐u] = c[α⇐u][γ⇐w[α⇐u]], for γ different from α and not free in u. In the identity for two names the case that matters is a named term [γ]r: both sides are [γ](r″ w[α⇐u]), with r″ the two sides for r; for [α]r both sides are [α](r″ u), because γ is not free in u. The remaining two have a substitution in the place of the outer appending. Substitution at two variables: c[w/y][u/x] = c[u/x][w[u/x]/y], for y different from x and not free in u. Substitution passes through appending: c[γ⇐w][u/x] = c[u/x][γ⇐w[u/x]], for γ not free in u.

Now let t → t′, by induction on where the step is. If the step is a β step of the whole term, t = (λx.s) w, then t[α⇐u] = (λx.s[α⇐u]) w[α⇐u], which takes a β step to s[α⇐u][w[α⇐u]/x], and that is s[w/x][α⇐u], because appending passes through a substitution. If it is a μ step of the whole term, t = (μγ.[β]s) w, then t[α⇐u] = (μγ.([β]s)[α⇐u]) w[α⇐u], which takes a μ step to μγ.([β]s)[α⇐u][γ⇐w[α⇐u]], and that is (μγ.([β]s)[γ⇐w])[α⇐u], by the identity for appending at two names. If the step is inside a subterm of t, the induction applies to that subterm, and appending keeps the subterm in its place, with at most u put after it. For t[u/x] the proof is the same, with the two identities that have a substitution outside.

If u → u′: t[α⇐u] contains one copy of u for each named term [α]w that was changed, and one step in each copy leads to t[α⇐u′]; there may be no copy. In the same way t[u/x] contains one copy of u for each free x.

Names. Neither rule looks at a free name: a β step and a μ step of t are steps of t[α/α′], with the bound names chosen different from α, and putting α for α′ passes through a substitution and through appending at a bound name. So a run from t gives a run of the same length from t[α/α′].

R1 and R2. R1 as in {{head}}. For R2: no step can change the variable that is applied, because each rule needs an abstraction or a μ-abstraction in front of an argument. So the term stays a variable applied to arguments, and every step is inside one of the arguments.

Under μ. The term μα.[β]t is not applied to anything, and no rule acts on a name. So every step of it is a step inside t, and its runs are the runs of t.

R3. For the β head step the proof of {{head}} holds, with the item steps kept for the two facts it uses about t[u/x]. For the μ head step, write s = (μα.[β]t) u u1…un and s′ = (μα.([β]t)[α⇐u]) u1…un. The subterms t, u, u1,…,un are in SN: u by assumption; each ui as a subterm of s′; and t because every step of t is a step of t[α⇐u], by the item steps kept, and t[α⇐u] is a subterm of s′.

Take any sequence of steps from s. As long as the head step is not taken, the term keeps the form (μα.[β]t1) u′ u1′…un′ and each step is inside one of these subterms, and those stop. If the head step is taken, the result is (μα.([β]t1)[α⇐u′]) u1′…un′, which s′ reaches by steps: both halves of the item steps kept say so for the μ-abstraction in front, and the arguments are reached one by one. And s′ ∈ SN. Either way the sequence is finite. ∎

Remark · What changed, and what did not

Worked out for this page, with the lemma. The remark “What the proof asked of terms” in {{rules}} lists what the main line uses about terms. These lines of it hold for λμ-terms, by this section: Lemma R, a head step that survives one more argument, the β head step of an abstraction, and the three facts about Base. The last line of that list, that a member of cl(S) reaches S by safe head steps, is the definition of cl(S), read for both sorts of head step. What is new is one more sort of head step and one more form of term with no head step, the μ-abstraction with no argument. That form is where {{classical}} finds the difference: an abstraction with no argument is put into a set by the arrow, which looks at what the abstraction does to each argument, and a μ-abstraction can be given any number of arguments, so no single arrow looks at all of what it does. It is also where some earlier lemmas stop as they stand: the head steps of a strongly normalizing term may now end at a μ-abstraction, which is neither an abstraction nor in Base, so the lemmas of {{structure}} and {{families}} that say “an abstraction or a member of Base” were not read again and do not hold word for word.

Not done on this page, and named so that a reader does not look for it. The generation lemma of {{gen}} for λμ-terms, which would have five rules, one for each form; the reader of the papers of van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro and of David and Nour derived it and checked it by program, and it has had no second reader. The characterization of the strongly normalizing λμ-terms by intersection types, which is the theorem of the paper of van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro; the remark “What is in the literature, and what was left out” in {{classical}} reports it.
