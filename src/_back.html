Status of the claims

Each lemma and theorem carries a one-word status in its header: textbook, quoted from a listed source, here, reviewed for a result worked out for this page and re-derived by an independent reader, or here, unreviewed. This list gives the same information by topic, with what the one word cannot say.

  • Textbook. The definition of a type set, the arrow lemma, the intersection lemma, the theorem and both corollaries. This is the short form of Girard’s proof that uses Tait’s conditions. The definition of a reducibility candidate. The Church–Rosser theorem, the standardization theorem and subject reduction for the kinding rules of Fω, each quoted where it is used. That head steps commute with other steps, in {{families}}, is part of the proof of the standardization theorem; the proof given there is this page’s.
  • From the listed sources. The content of Harper’s note. The Mendler-style encoding, the identity mappings and casts, and the claims about Cedille’s and Church’s predecessor, from Firsov, Blair and Stump. What {{erasure}} says about Cedille’s erasure, from the paper on Cedille Core. That Girard’s conditions are stronger than Tait’s. That unions of candidates are candidates, with the quotation on what was believed before, is Riba’s theorem; the two-line proof for this setting in {{cmpsets}} is this page’s. The two self-type rules. What the remark on self types reports about System S and about the Calculus of Dependent Lambda Eliminations, including its two quotations. The rules for the implicit product and for the equality, quoted from the paper on Cedille Core with the terms erased. What Stump’s semantics does, and the two quotations on normalization, from his paper on the syntax and semantics of Cedille. The characterization theorem of {{andsn}}, which secondary sources attribute to Coppo and Dezani for terms that never discard an argument and to Pottinger for all terms; Krivine’s book treats System D, and its theorem numbers were not checked. What {{and}} says about ω, from Kikuchi’s paper, and what {{andlaws}} says about intersection types that count uses, from Bernadet and Graham-Lengrand. The form ιx:A.B and its two rules, which are Kopylov’s.
  • From general knowledge, and not checked against sources for this page. The table of other semantics in {{cmpsem}}, including which definition each grows from, except what is attributed to Riba. Reynolds’s theorem and Pitts’s reply. What {{strength}} says about how strong a proof has to be. What {{cube}} says about Geuvers and Nederhof and about universes. That some strongly normalizing terms are not typable in System F. That no program decides strong normalization, or whether a term has a normal form. The attribution of saturated sets to Tait’s paper of 1975, and of the generalization to Mitchell. The two claims about subject reduction in {{limits}}. That erasing the annotations of the term of Girard, Coquand or Hurkens leaves a term that is not in SN. That the route of the proof in {{andsn}}, induction on the generation lemma, is known. What {{families}} says about the sets built from values in the semantics of programming languages and in proofs by tests, and what the remark of {{tests}} says about tests as continuations in Parigot’s and Krivine’s work.
  • Worked out for this page, and re-derived line by line by an independent reader. The proof of the generation lemma, which is a known fact. The union lemma. The characterization theorem’s proof in {{andsn}}. The lemma on the dependent intersection. The implicit product and Cedille’s natural numbers, which follow Stump’s construction with the argument order of this page and with P over families on all terms, where he has predicates on the Church numerals. The equality type set, the counterexamples to ρ and φ, and the derivation of a closed term outside SN. The lemma that type sets tell terms apart. In {{families}}: the chain, the commuting lemma’s proof, the four conversions, the correspondence between head-closed type sets and sets of values, the lemma on complements, and the lemma that each collection carries the theorem. The addition lemma of {{price}} and its two extensions. The lemma on ER in {{equalities}}. The argument on the weakly normalizing terms in {{good}} and the argument on Church style in {{rules}}. Both lemmas of {{induct}}, with the counts of head steps. The erasure lemma of {{erasure}}. The lemma of {{hyp}}. The lemma of {{impred}}. The lemma of {{pairs}}, the abstraction theorem and the two free theorems, including the claim that the main line uses nothing else about terms, which the reader checked against the proofs. Both lemmas of {{tests}}.
  • Worked out for this page, and checked by an earlier independent reader. The alternative with n ≤ 1 in narrow point 2, and the counterexample in narrow point 4. The Empty example. The hand-built type sets Gen(S) and the examples that use them. Lemma E, Lemma L and the picture of trees of head steps. The counting lemma of {{allsets}}. The proof that candidates are the type sets with CR2, and the two-line proof on unions. The self-type lemma and the Nat construction in this semantics, where P ranges over families on all terms, which differs from Fu and Stump’s own system; whether the rule of System S for recursive definitions allows more than monotone recursion was not checked. The dependent arrow and EQ, Lemma N extended to terms inside type expressions, and the theorem on the rules for dependent types. The laws of ∩ and the law that is true and not derivable. The four limits of {{limits}}. The proof in narrow point 7, the remark on two readings of kinds, and the account of what each feature of the λ-cube costs.
  • Worked out for this page, and not reviewed. The examples “A family that no program can decide” in {{fam}}, “The discarded argument has to be typable” in {{andsn}}, “Applying a dependent function” in {{dep}} and the Mendler-style natural numbers in {{induct}} were checked by the last reader; the tables, remarks and bullet lists of {{induct}}, {{erasure}}, {{hyp}}, {{impred}} and {{pairs}} were read by that reader and corrected where wrong, and the corrections themselves have not been read by anyone else. The list in {{fam}} of what each construction asks of a function from terms to type sets, and the remark in {{rules}} on what the proof asked of the collection that ∀ ranges over, were both corrected after that review and not re-read, and so were the remark in {{rules}} on what the proof asked of terms and the paragraph on proof-irrelevant propositions in {{equalities}}, the example on negation in {{ops}} and the note on complements in {{families}}, which were written afterwards. {{tests}} was written after that review and then re-derived by a further reader, who also supplied the proof that every closed term fails some test; the corrections made after that reading have not been read by anyone else.
  • Open on this page. Whether some closed type expression names different sets under the three collections of {{families}}; for ∀X.X it does not. Whether the inductive part of a Mendler-style fixed point in {{induct}} can be built without the least fixed point. Whether a loop over the members of a recursive type can be typed, in the table of {{induct}}. Whether the test-defined sets of {{tests}} are closed under union.
  • Errors found by review and fixed. The last review, of the five sections written after the one before it, found and the page corrected: a claim that only term conversion needs families constant along all steps, where “∀ out, for families” needs it too; a lemma on intersections whose statement left out the index sets that are sets of terms; a table row that called the proof algebras impredicative; a table column that mixed two encodings of the numbers; an attribution of Cedille’s casts to the rule φ, where the paper uses an annotated intersection that Cedille Core later replaced by φ; a claim that being the same term does not survive substitution; a wrong reason for the Base condition; an incomplete list of what the proof asks of the collection that ∀ ranges over; a conclusion about naming drawn from undecidability; an unneeded hypothesis; a false sentence about pairs in Base; and an overstatement about head-closed relations. The two reviews before it found, and the page corrected: a justification in {{families}} that did not prove its claim, about ID being a candidate, replaced by an example that the section proves; a false claim that the candidates need a family constant along steps for the dependent arrow; a false claim that two terms with the same evaluation stay so under steps; a claim, stated as fact, that type expressions name different sets under different collections, which is open; three inconsistent accounts of Riba’s theorem; a sentence in {{structure}} that said no later proof uses Lemma E or Lemma L; an overclaim that no semantics proving strong normalization can make φ true, which holds for β and φ together; a one-sided hypothesis in the lemma on ER; an example of a non-substitutive relation that was wrong; a false claim that no rule relates ∀X.X and ∀X.∀Y.X; and a retracted claim that the saturated sets give stronger parametricity.
  • Not on this page. Named here so that a reader does not look for them. Classical logic, the λμ-calculus and Krivine’s realizability, beyond the collection of sets they start from in {{tests}}. Function extensionality, quotient types, observational equality, homotopy type theory and cubical type theory, beyond the home that {{pairs}} names for the first three. Universes beyond the levels of {{allkinds}} and the sentence in {{cube}}. Inductive types with indices, and the inductive types of proof assistants with their own forms of terms, which limit 4 of {{limits}} covers in one line. Strong normalization of annotated terms for the dependent corners of the λ-cube, which {{erasure}} reduces to one missing argument. System S and Cedille as whole systems, as against the constructions of theirs that {{self}}, {{dinter}}, {{implicit}}, {{equality}} and {{induct}} try. Subject reduction, which {{limits}} quotes. Any rule for well-formed type expressions, and anything a machine could check.
  • The order of the sections. The division into sections, the “Uses” lines and the graph at the top were made for this page. A program checked that every earlier section that a text refers to is among the sections it uses, apart from passing mentions such as the number of a narrow point. No second reader has checked them.
