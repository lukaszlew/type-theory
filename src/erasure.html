Every term on this page is untyped, and every rule leaves the term as it is or builds it from the terms of the premises. The usual presentations write type expressions inside terms: λx:A.t, ΛX.t, t [B], and more for the larger systems. The remark on Church style in {{rules}} said how the theorem reaches such terms for System F. This section states the argument once, as a lemma with one hypothesis, and lists what each annotated form on this page erases to. It is the one place where the page meets the terms that proof assistants check.

Definition · Erasure, loud and silent steps

An annotated calculus is a set of annotated terms, together with steps of its own between them and with an erasure.

The erasure of an annotated term t, written |t|, is a term of {{terms}}.

A step of the annotated calculus from t to t′ is loud if |t| → |t′| is a step, and silent if |t| = |t′|.

The calculus is erasable if every one of its steps is loud or silent, and no annotated term has an infinite run of silent steps.

Erasure lemma · No infinite run passes from erasures to annotated termshere, reviewed

Let the annotated calculus be erasable, and let t be an annotated term with |t| ∈ SN. Then t has no infinite run.

So if, for every derivable judgment about an annotated term t, the erased judgment about |t| is derivable in a system whose rules this page has shown true, then no typable annotated term has an infinite run.

Proof

Suppose t has an infinite run. If only finitely many of its steps are loud, then from some point on every step is silent, and that is an infinite run of silent steps, which the calculus does not have. So infinitely many steps are loud, and each loud step is a step of the erasure, while a silent step leaves the erasure unchanged. Erasing the whole run gives an infinite run of |t|, against |t| ∈ SN.

For the second statement, C1 (strong normalization) of the erased system gives |t| ∈ SN, and the first statement gives the claim. ∎

Two things have to be checked for each annotated calculus, and they are the whole content of the lemma: that every step is loud or silent, and that silent runs end. Remark · The other route: do not erase

Quoted from Geuvers’s paper of 1994. His proof of strong normalization for the Calculus of Constructions keeps the annotations and changes the terms that the sets are made of. There is one language for terms, type expressions and kinds: variables, λv:T.M, Πv:T.U, application, and two constants. A saturated set is a set of such annotated terms with the three conditions of {{typeset}}, restated for them. The set that every saturated set contains has a variable, either of the two constants, or a Πv:M.N with M and N strongly normalizing, each applied to any number of strongly normalizing arguments. The closure condition lets (λv:N.M) P Q1…Qn in if M[P/v] Q1…Qn is a member, and P is strongly normalizing, and so is the annotation N. A kind is read in two ways at once: as a plain set, which says what a variable of that kind ranges over, and as a saturated set, which has the type expressions of that kind as members; the saturated set of the kind of all type expressions is the set of all strongly normalizing annotated terms. The soundness theorem then has the shape of the one in {{rules}}, a typable annotated term with its variables replaced by members is a member, and every term, type expression and kind is strongly normalizing, by replacing each variable by itself. He points out that the proof uses two facts about derivations and no more, and that, with the rule of conversion taken one step at a time, neither rests on subject reduction; nor is there a translation into another system.

Measured against this page. That is limit 4 of {{limits}} taken up and not the erasure lemma: annotated terms are terms with new forms, Lemma R and the three conditions are restated for them, and no step is silent, because the sets see the annotations. One more condition appears, of the same sort as “safe”: an annotation has to be strongly normalizing before a head step may be undone. His reading of type expressions is the forgetful one of {{forget}}. This page does not carry the route out. It is the argument missing from the third row of the table, done another way, and its two readings of a kind at once are the two readings that {{allkinds}} keeps apart.
