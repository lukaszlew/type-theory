With the λμ-terms of {{mu}} the definitions of {{typeset}}, {{ops}} and {{tests}} can be read again, word for word. The lemma “What carries over to λμ-terms” lists what was read again with them and stays true by the same proofs. The rules of System F stay true as well. One rule is new, the rule that makes a μ-abstraction, and with it Peirce’s law has a proof. With the three conditions of {{typeset}} alone that rule is false. This section gives a fourth condition under which it is true; every set defined by tests has it, and the smallest type set has not. Whether a weaker fourth condition would do was not examined. So the rule μ is the first rule on this page that is true over a smaller collection of sets and false over the largest. Parigot proved strong normalization for second-order classical natural deduction, the system of proofs that these terms and rules are taken from, in papers of 1993 and 1997; they could not be obtained for this page. The statements and proofs here were worked out for this page by readers of later papers, which the remark “What is in the literature, and what was left out” quotes.

Definition · Type sets and tests of λμ-terms

In this section a term is a λμ-term, and steps, SN, Base, head steps, “safe” and “closed” are those of {{mu}}.

A type set is a set of terms with the three conditions of {{typeset}}, the closure condition being read for both sorts of head step.

The arrow T ⇒ U is defined by the words of {{obstacle}}, and tests, “passes”, P⊥ and “test-defined” by the words of {{tests}}.

In this section TT is the collection of the test-defined sets of λμ-terms.

A test of a type set T is a test that every member of T passes.

The empty test is a test of every type set, by the SN condition. If T = P⊥, then every test in P is a test of T, and T may have more tests than those.

Lemma · What carries over to λμ-termshere, reviewed

operations
The arrow lemma, the intersection lemma and the union lemma of {{ops}} and the four facts of {{facts}} hold for type sets of λμ-terms.
tests
Every test-defined set is a type set. If T and U are test-defined, then so is T ⇒ U, and the intersection of a nonempty set of test-defined sets is test-defined. The set of the terms that pass every test is the smallest test-defined set, and λx.y is a member of it.
against a test
Let v⃗ be a test in whose terms α is not free. The μ-abstraction μα.[β]t passes v⃗ if and only if the term inside ([β]t)[α⇐v⃗] is in SN: that term is t[α⇐v⃗] if β is not α, and t[α⇐v⃗] v⃗ if β is α.

The item against a test is the whole of what a μ-abstraction does: given a test, it appends the test at its name α, and it passes if what then stands under the name in front is strongly normalizing. Not read again, and so not claimed: the lemmas of {{structure}} and of {{families}}, and the rest of {{tests}}; the remark “What changed, and what did not” in {{mu}} says where some of them stop.

Proof

Operations. The proofs in {{ops}} and {{facts}} use, about terms, R1 (subterms), that a variable is in Base and a member of Base applied to a member of SN is in Base, that a head step survives one more argument, and the β head step of an abstraction applied to an argument. {{mu}} has all of these for λμ-terms. Where one of those proofs undoes a safe head step, it does so by the closure condition of a type set, which is now read for both sorts of head step.

Tests. The proof of the item candidates, as far as the three conditions, and of the item arrow and intersection of the lemma “Test-defined sets form a fourth collection” in {{tests}} reads word for word, with R3 (safe head step) of {{mu}}. The intersection of all test-defined sets is the set of terms that pass every test, by the proof of the item not empty, and its ends there. And λx.y passes every test by the proof of the item passes all in {{tests}}, which uses R2 and R3 only.

Against a test. Let v⃗ = v1,…,vn. The term (μα.[β]t) v1…vn reaches μα.([β]t)[α⇐v⃗] by n head steps, with the arguments v1,…,vn, because appending one term after another is appending the list, by the note in {{mu}}: α is not free in the terms of the test. The arguments are in SN, so the head steps are safe. If the μ-abstraction passes, the term reached is in SN, by R1 (subterms). If the term reached is in SN, the μ-abstraction passes, by R3 (safe head step) n times. And the term reached is in SN if and only if the term inside its named term is, by the item under μ of Lemma R for λμ-terms. ∎

Definition · Replacing variables and names at once

Let a term ui be given for each of the distinct variables x1,…,xn, and a list of terms v⃗j for each of the distinct names α1,…,αm.

The replacement of a term t puts ui for every free xi and appends v⃗j at every free αj, all in one pass: a named term [αj]w becomes [αj](w′ v⃗j), where w′ is w replaced in the same way.

Nothing is done inside the terms put in, and the bound variables and bound names of t are first chosen so that they are not free in those terms.

The terms put in may have the names αj free; that is why the replacement is one pass and not one appending after another. With one name and no variable it is the appending of {{mu}}.

Definition · Judgments with names

A name context Δ is a list α1:B1, …, αm:Bm of distinct names, each with a type expression of System F.

A judgment now consists of a context Γ, a term t, a type expression A and a name context Δ.

Fix a nonempty collection of type sets for ∀ to range over and for assignments to take their values in. The judgment is true for that collection, written Γ ⊨ t : A | Δ, if the following holds: for every assignment ρ, every choice of a member ui of ⟦Ai⟧ρ for each xi:Ai of Γ, and every choice of a test v⃗j of ⟦Bj⟧ρ for each αj:Bj of Δ, the replacement of t with these terms is in ⟦A⟧ρ.

A variable stands for a member of a set, and a name stands for a test of a set: for something that waits for a member, which the literature calls a continuation.

Example · One judgment, with its replacement

Take the judgment with the context x : X, the term μγ.[α]x, the type expression Y and the name context α : X. For a member u of ρ(X) and a test v⃗ of ρ(X), the replacement of the term is μγ.[α](u v⃗), with γ chosen so that it is not free in u and v⃗. The judgment is true if that term is in ρ(Y) for every such choice. The rule μ will derive this judgment, and the theorem “The rules with μ are true over sets with the μ condition” says for which collections it is true.

Definition · The rules, with the rule for μ

The rules are the five rules of {{rules}}, each with a name context that is the same above and below the line, and with “∀ in” asking that X is free neither in Γ nor in Δ; and one more rule, called μ:

rulefromconclude
μΓ ⊢ t : B | Δ, where Δ has β:B and α:AΓ ⊢ μα.[β]t : A | Δ′, where Δ′ is Δ without α:A

As in {{rules}}, a judgment is derivable if it is the last judgment of a derivation, and Γ ⊢ t : A | Δ says that it is.

A name context may list names that are not free in the term, as a context may list variables that are not. In the rule, β may be α, and then B is A. The rule reads: to show A, suppose that a test of the set of A is given, call it α, and produce a term for which one of the tests at hand is waiting. Quoted: the rule is Parigot’s, in the form in which van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro give his system for arrows; the two rules for ∀ are those of {{rules}}, in which the term does not change, and Rozière’s lecture notes say the same of the second-order system.

Example · Peirce’s law

Write Γ for y : (X → Y) → X.

linejudgmentrule
1Γ, x:X ⊢ x : X | α:X, γ:Yvariable
2Γ, x:X ⊢ μγ.[α]x : Y | α:Xμ: x is named α, and γ is bound
3Γ ⊢ λx.μγ.[α]x : X → Y | α:X→ in
4Γ ⊢ y (λx.μγ.[α]x) : X | α:Xvariable, then → out with 3
5Γ ⊢ μα.[α](y (λx.μγ.[α]x)) : X |μ: named α, and α is bound
6⊢ λy.μα.[α](y (λx.μγ.[α]x)) : ((X → Y) → X) → X |→ in

The term of line 3 is a function that throws away whatever is waiting for its result, the test γ, and puts its argument in front of the test α. If y is replaced by λk.k v, a function that calls its argument with v, the term of line 5 reaches μα.[α](μγ.[α]v): the term v has been given to α, from inside the call. If α is not free in v, the rule that renames and the rule that removes, both left out in {{mu}}, would take that term on to v.

Quoted: the term is the usual proof of Peirce’s law in this calculus; it is in Rozière’s lecture notes, with a derivation in his rules, which name a term and bind a name in two separate steps.

Definition · The μ condition, and two sets

A type set T has the μ condition if every μ-abstraction that passes every test of T is a member of T.

MU is the collection of the type sets of λμ-terms that have the μ condition.

T0 is the set of the strongly normalizing terms whose head steps lead to a member of Base.

T1 is the set of the strongly normalizing terms whose head steps do not lead to an abstraction.

“Head steps lead to” is meant with zero or more head steps; a term has at most one head step, so there is one sequence of them, and for a strongly normalizing term it ends, at an abstraction, at a μ-abstraction or at a variable applied to arguments. A type set is test-defined exactly if every term that passes every test of the set is a member: one direction is the note on the orthogonal of an orthogonal in {{tests}}, and for the other the tests of the set are a nonempty set of tests, the empty test being one. The μ condition asks the same of the μ-abstractions only.

Example · One set that has the μ condition, and one that has not

SN has the μ condition: the empty test is a test of SN, and a μ-abstraction that passes it is in SN. The set T0 has not, because of the term μα.[α]x: applied to the terms of any test it reaches μα.[α](x v⃗), which is in SN, and it has no head step and is not in Base. The item not the smallest of the lemma “Which type sets have the μ condition” gives the proof.

Lemma · Which type sets have the μ conditionhere, reviewed

tests suffice
Every test-defined set has the μ condition.
not the smallest
T0 is the smallest type set of λμ-terms, and it does not have the μ condition: μα.[α]x passes every test and is not a member.
not only tests
T1 is a type set with the μ condition, it is closed under steps, and it is not test-defined.
operations
SN has the μ condition. If T is a type set and U is a type set with the μ condition, then T ⇒ U has the μ condition. The intersection of a nonempty set of type sets with the μ condition has it.

So TT is inside MU, the two differ, and MU is not the collection of all type sets. The item tests suffice, and the half of the item not the smallest that says that T0 does not have the μ condition, were found by three readers of the papers named in the remark “What is in the literature, and what was left out”, each without knowing of the others. That T0 is the smallest type set, and the item not only tests with its set and its proof, are from the reader who first reviewed this section; it answers a question that an earlier form of this section left open, whether a type set with the μ condition that is closed under steps has to be test-defined.

Proof

Tests suffice. Let T = P⊥. Every test in P is a test of T. So a term that passes every test of T passes every test in P, and is in P⊥.

Not the smallest. T0 is a type set. SN condition: by the definition. Base condition: a member of Base is in SN by R2 (variable applied), and zero head steps lead to it. Closure condition: if s →h s′ is safe and s′ ∈ T0, then s ∈ SN by R3 (safe head step), and the head steps of s are that step and then those of s′. It is the smallest: let t ∈ T0. The terms on the way from t to the member of Base are in SN, by R1 (subterms), and the argument of each head step is a subterm of the term the step is taken from, so every one of these head steps is safe. A type set contains the member of Base and is closed under undoing safe head steps, so it contains t. Now the term μα.[α]x. For a test v⃗, the term x v⃗ is in SN by R2 (variable applied), so the μ-abstraction passes v⃗, by the item against a test. It has no head step and is not in Base, so it is not in T0.

Not only tests. T1 is a type set by the same three checks: a member of Base has no head step and is not an abstraction. It has the μ condition: a μ-abstraction that passes every test of T1 passes the empty test, so it is in SN; it has no head step; and it is not an abstraction. It is not test-defined: λx.y is in every test-defined set, by the item tests of the lemma on what carries over, and it is an abstraction.

It is closed under steps. First, head steps and other steps, which is the first statement of the lemma “Head steps commute with other steps” of {{families}} with the μ head step added: if t →h s and t → t′ by a step that is not the head step, then t′ →h s′ for a term s′ that s reaches by zero or more steps. For the step is inside the abstraction or μ-abstraction in front or inside an argument, so t′ has the form of t, and the term after its head step is reached from s, by the item steps kept of Lemma R for λμ-terms. Second, for a strongly normalizing t write e(t) for the form of the term that the head steps of t end at: abstraction, μ-abstraction, or variable applied to arguments. Claim: if t ∈ SN and t → t′, then e(t′) = e(t). By induction on the length of the longest run from t, which exists because a term has finitely many steps. If t has no head step, the step is inside it, and t′ has the form of t and no head step. If t →h s and t′ is s, then e(t′) = e(t) by the definition. Otherwise t′ →h s′ and s reaches s′; the induction hypothesis, used along the steps from s to s′, gives e(s′) = e(s), and e(t′) = e(s′) and e(t) = e(s) by the definition. So if t ∈ T1 and t → t′, then t′ ∈ SN by R1 (subterms), and e(t′) = e(t) is not “abstraction”.

Operations. SN is test-defined, by the one empty test. Let m be a μ-abstraction that passes every test of T ⇒ U, and let u ∈ T. The term m u takes a head step to a μ-abstraction m′, and the step is safe because u ∈ SN. For every test w⃗ of U, the list u, w⃗ is a test of T ⇒ U: if s ∈ T ⇒ U, then s u ∈ U, so s u w⃗ ∈ SN. So m u w⃗ ∈ SN, and m′ w⃗ ∈ SN by R1 (subterms). So m′ passes every test of U, and m′ ∈ U by the μ condition. The closure condition of U gives m u ∈ U. So m ∈ T ⇒ U. For an intersection: a test of one of the sets is passed by every member of the intersection, so it is a test of the intersection; so a μ-abstraction that passes every test of the intersection passes every test of each set, and is a member of each. ∎

Lemma · Closed λμ-terms fail a test, and a free name is enough to pass them allhere, reviewed

closed terms fail
For every closed term t there is a test v⃗ that t does not pass.
a free name passes
The term μα.[γ]I has no free variable, and it passes every test.

So “closed” has to forbid free names as well as free variables. The item closed terms fail was found by three readers independently and checked by programs, the last time on every closed normal form with at most ten symbols; the proof given here is the writer’s wording of theirs.

Proof

A free name passes. α does not occur in [γ]I, so each head step of (μα.[γ]I) v1…vn throws one argument away, and n safe head steps lead to the normal form μα.[γ]I. So the term is in SN, by R3 (safe head step).

Closed terms fail. If t ∉ SN, t does not pass the empty test. Let t ∈ SN, and let t̂ be a normal form that t reaches. It is closed, because no step adds a free variable or a free name. By the five forms of {{mu}}, a normal form is a variable applied to normal forms, or λx. in front of a normal form, or μα.[β] in front of a normal form. So t̂ is a list of prefixes, each λx. or μα.[β], in front of a term x u1…um. The variable x is bound by one of the prefixes λx., and the name β of a prefix μα.[β] is bound by that prefix or by a prefix μβ. to its left. Let k be the number of prefixes λ, and let N = k + m + 1.

Apply t̂ to any N strongly normalizing terms v1,…,vN and take steps prefix by prefix, from the left. Keep a number q, at first 0: what is left of t̂ is applied to vq+1,…,vN. At a prefix λy., one β step puts vq+1 for y, and q grows by one. At a prefix μα.[β], the μ steps append vq+1,…,vN at α; record qα = q. What is left now stands under the name β, where it is followed by the list that was appended at β, which is vqβ+1,…,vN; so q becomes qβ, which is q itself if β is α. The steps after a prefix μα.[β] are steps inside the term. The number q never exceeds the number of prefixes λ passed, so q ≤ k and the terms never run out. After the last prefix the term contains vp u1′…um′ vq+1…vN, where p ≤ k is the number that q reached at the prefix that binds x, and the ui′ are the ui after these replacements. The numbers p and q depend on t̂ only.

Now choose the test: vp = λz1.…λzm.δ, and vi = δ for every other i. All are normal forms. Then vp u1′…um′ reaches δ by m head steps, and the term contains δ vq+1. If q + 1 is not p, that is δ δ = Ω. If q + 1 is p and m = 0, then vp is δ, and again it is Ω. If q + 1 is p and m ≥ 1, then δ vp reaches vp vp, which is followed by vp+1,…,vN; the first vp takes m arguments, vp, vp+1,…,vp+m−1, and becomes δ, which is followed by vp+m = δ; there are enough terms because p + m ≤ k + m < N. In every case t̂ v⃗ reaches a term that contains Ω, so it is not in SN, by R1 (subterms); and the steps from t to t̂ are steps of t v⃗.

One instance of the last case: for μβ.[β]λx.μα.[β](x x) the numbers are k = 1, m = 1, N = 3, p = 1 and, at the end, q = 0. The test is λz.δ, δ, δ, and the term under the names reaches v1 v1 v1 v2 v3, then δ v1 v2 v3, then v1 v1 v2 v3, then δ δ δ. ∎

Example · The proof of Peirce’s law fails a test

The closed term of the example on Peirce’s law is a normal form. It does not pass the test λz.δ, δ: with λz.δ for y and then δ appended at α, the term reaches μα.[α]((λz.δ) (λx.μγ.[α](x δ)) δ), and then μα.[α](δ δ). A program that searches all runs confirmed it.

Theorem · The rules with μ are true over sets with the μ conditionhere, reviewed

In the items soundness, C1 and C2 the collection that ∀ ranges over is TT or MU.

soundness
If Γ ⊢ t : A | Δ, then Γ ⊨ t : A | Δ for the collection.
C1
Strong normalization. If Γ ⊢ t : A | Δ, then t ∈ SN.
C2
Consistency. No closed term t has ⊢ t : ∀X.X |, with both contexts empty.
not over all type sets
The judgment x : X ⊢ μα.[α]x : X | is derivable, and it is false for the collection of all type sets.

C1 is, for the rules β and μ, Parigot’s theorem on strong normalization; the proof was made for this page. The item not over all type sets says exactly this: with the three conditions of {{typeset}} alone, read for λμ-terms as in this section, the new rule is false.

Proof

Both collections are not empty, consist of type sets, and are closed under ⇒ and under intersections of nonempty sets of members: TT by the item tests of the lemma on what carries over, MU by the item operations of the lemma on the μ condition and the arrow and intersection lemmas. So Lemma N and the four facts hold, by the remark “What the proof asked of the collection that ∀ ranges over” in {{rules}}.

Soundness. By induction on the derivation. Fix ρ, members for the variables of Γ and tests for the names of Δ, and write t* for the replacement of t with them. The five rules of {{rules}} go as in the proof there. In “→ in” the term (λx.t)* u takes a safe head step to t* with u put for x, which is the replacement of t with u for x added, because the bound x is not free in the terms put in. In “∀ in” the sets of the type expressions in Δ do not change while ρ(X) varies, as those in Γ do not, so the chosen tests stay tests.

The rule μ. The judgment below the line has the name context Δ′, so tests are given for the names of Δ′. Let v⃗ be a test of ⟦A⟧ρ. Choose a name α′ that is not a name of Δ and is not free in t, in the terms put in, or in the terms of v⃗; write t′ for t[α′/α], and β′ for β, or for α′ if β is α. Then (μα.[β]t)* is m = μα′.([β′]t′)*, and by the item against a test it passes v⃗ if the term inside ([β′]t′)*[α′⇐v⃗] is in SN; call that term r′. Because α′ is not free in the terms put in, appending at α′ finds nothing to do inside them, so ([β′]t′)*[α′⇐v⃗] is the replacement of [β′]t′ with v⃗ added for α′.

Now take the judgment above the line, with the same members, the same tests for the names of Δ′, and v⃗ for α. It is true, so the replacement of t with these, written t**, is in ⟦B⟧ρ. Let r be t** followed by the test for β, which is v⃗ itself if β is α; in both cases that is a test of ⟦B⟧ρ, so r ∈ SN. So r′ and r are two replacements with the same terms, of t′ and of t, each followed by the same test; and α′ occurs in r′ only where t′ has it. So r is r′[α/α′], and r′ ∈ SN, by the item names of Lemma R for λμ-terms. So m passes every test of ⟦A⟧ρ, and m ∈ ⟦A⟧ρ by the μ condition.

C1. Take any assignment ρ into the collection, which is not empty. Replace each variable by itself, which the Base condition allows, and append the empty test at each name, which is a test of every type set. The term does not change, so t ∈ ⟦A⟧ρ ⊆ SN.

C2. The set of the terms that pass every test is test-defined, so it is a member of both collections, and the set that ∀X.X names is inside it. By the item closed terms fail of the lemma “Closed λμ-terms fail a test”, it has no closed member.

Not over all type sets. x : X ⊢ x : X | α:X by the variable rule, and the rule μ, with β the name α, gives the judgment. Send X to T0 and replace x by itself: μα.[α]x is not in T0, by the item not the smallest. ∎

Remark · What is in the literature, and what was left out

Quoted, each from the paper named; Parigot’s own papers were not read. Nour and Saber write that their semantics “is inspired by the strong normalization proof of Parigot’s λµ-calculus, which consists in rewriting each reducibility candidate as a double orthogonal”, and Battyányi and Nour describe his proof in the same way. So, on these accounts, his sets are sets defined by tests; whether they are all of them, or the smallest collection of them that the proof needs, the two papers do not say. In both papers a name with a type expression is replaced by a finite list taken from the orthogonal of the set of that type expression, as here; in Battyányi and Nour it is an initial part of such a list, and the lists of Nour and Saber may hold names as well as terms. Both papers write the terms in the other way, in which μα may stand in front of any term. Van Bakel, Barbanera and de’Liguoro do the same for a system with intersection types and no ∀: a type expression for names stands for a set of “stacks”, finite lists of strongly normalizing terms, and a type expression for terms stands for the terms t with t v⃗ ∈ SN for every stack v⃗ of such a set. Their theorem: a λμ-term is strongly normalizing, for β and μ, if and only if it is typable in their system. Here: that is the counterpart of {{andsn}}. Their system has a rule of subtyping, which {{andsn}} did without, and their proof that the strongly normalizing terms are typable goes by undoing the leftmost step. Worked out for this page: the theorem is quoted without its proof having been checked. A reader of that paper for this page found their Lemma 2.17, the lemma that says that the rules are true for their sets, false as printed, at the rule for abstraction with ω for the stack: the rules give λx.x a type expression whose set does not contain it. Both reviewers of this section confirmed that by hand. That reader has a repair, which no one else has read.

Matthes reaches strong normalization by another route, with no sets: second-order classical natural deduction, with annotated terms, is translated into System F so that every step becomes one or more steps. He reports that such translations, his own and those that pass to every term what is waiting for it, are not correct for terms without annotations, and that Nakazawa and Tatsuta found the proofs by translations that pass to every term what is waiting for it to fail, among them the proof by translation in Parigot’s paper of 1997: a μ-abstraction whose name does not occur throws an argument away, and the steps inside that argument are lost in the translation.

Battyányi and Nour treat the rules that {{mu}} left out, for a system with arrows only. With the rule that renames, the rule that removes, and one more, strong normalization still holds. Of the mirror image of μ they say that their counterpart of the Base condition is trivial only because that rule is left out. Here: with the mirror image a variable applied to arguments can have a step at its head, so the proof of R2 (variable applied) no longer applies and a member of Base need not stay in Base. Quoted again: they report that the method of candidates does not give strong normalization there, and they quote the theorem, proved by other means, that β, μ and the mirror image together are strongly normalizing for typed terms; with the mirror image and the simplifying rules together they give a typed term with an infinite run.

Worked out for this page by readers of those papers, and not reviewed. In the way of writing terms in which μα may stand in front of any term, the closed term μα.I passes every test, because it throws every argument away; so the item closed terms fail is false there, and C2 (consistency) would need another proof. That is one reason for the way of writing terms chosen in {{mu}}.

Whether a condition weaker than the μ condition makes the rule true. Not on this page. Strong normalization with the rules that {{mu}} left out. Disjunction and existential types for λμ-terms, and the unions of {{tests}} read for them. The rules for a primitive ⊥; here falsity is ∀X.X.
