On this page a type expression names a set of terms. Reynolds’s parametricity reads a type expression as a relation between terms, and draws from the rules alone conclusions about what a term of a given type expression can do. This section shows that the relational reading costs one definition and no new proof: the main line of the page, {{typeset}} to {{rules}}, is the same theory run on pairs of terms. The abstraction theorem falls out, and two of its consequences are worked out.

Definition · Pairs, and type sets of pairs

A pair is two terms (t, t′), in order. Everything is done to both sides at once.

The variable x, as a pair, is (x, x).

The application (t, t′) (u, u′) is (t u, t′ u′).

The abstraction λx.(t, t′) is (λx.t, λx.t′).

The substitution (t, t′)[(u, u′)/x] is (t[u/x], t′[u′/x]).

A pair is in SN if both of its terms are, and it is closed if both of its terms are.

Base is the set of pairs (x u1…un, x v1…vn) with the same variable x and the same n on both sides, and all of u1,…,un, v1,…,vn in SN.

A pair takes a head step if both of its terms take one at once, and the step is safe if both arguments are in SN.

A relation is a set of pairs that satisfies the three conditions of {{typeset}}, read for pairs: inside SN, containing Base, closed under undoing safe head steps. The arrow R ⇒ S, the intersection over all relations, cl and Gen are defined by the same words as for terms, with “pair” for “term”.

A relation relates two terms if the pair is a member. R ⇒ S relates two functions if they send related arguments to related results. A pair with a variable at the head on one side only, or with different variables at the head of its two sides, is in no Base; and if only one side has a head step, the pair takes none. Such pairs are simply never produced by the constructions.

Lemma · The main line runs on pairshere, reviewed

The definitions and proofs of {{typeset}}, {{ops}}, {{facts}}, {{names}}, {{judg}} and {{rules}} hold with pairs in the place of terms and relations in the place of type sets, word for word. In particular, with X ranging over all relations: every type expression names a relation; and if Γ ⊢ t : A, then for every assignment ρ of relations to variables and every choice of pairs (ui, ui′) ∈ ⟦Ai⟧ρ, the pair (t[u⃗/x⃗], t[u⃗′/x⃗]) is in ⟦A⟧ρ.

abstraction theorem
If ⊢ t : A, then (t, t) ∈ ⟦A⟧ρ for every assignment ρ of relations.

The abstraction theorem is Reynolds’s, and the route through saturated relations is standard; the claim of this lemma is only that the proofs on this page need no change.

Proof

The remark in {{rules}} on what the proof asked of terms lists what the proofs of the sections named use about terms, and nothing else: R1 (subterms), R2 (variable applied) and R3 (safe head step) of Lemma R; that a head step survives one more argument, with the same argument; that (λx.t) u takes a head step to t[u/x] with argument u; that a variable is in Base, and a member of Base applied to a member of SN is in Base; that a member of Base has a free variable; and, for M2 (generated), that a member of cl(S) reaches S by safe head steps. Each holds for pairs, because each is checked on the two sides separately, and the two sides take their head steps together. The abstraction theorem is the soundness theorem read for pairs, in the case of an empty context, where there is nothing to replace. ∎

Example · Two free theorems

Let u, v, u′, v′ be closed strongly normalizing terms, and let no one of u, v reach the other by zero or more head steps, so in particular u and v differ, and likewise for u′, v′. The hypothesis is there so that “reaches u” and “reaches v” exclude each other.

  • Identity. If ⊢ t : ∀X.X→X, then t u reaches u by head steps. This needs no pairs: t ∈ ⋂C (C ⇒ C) by the soundness theorem of {{rules}}, so t u ∈ Gen({u}), so t u reaches u or a member of Base by head steps, by M2 (generated), and a closed term reaches no member of Base.
  • Choice. If ⊢ t : ∀X.X→X→X, then either t u v reaches u by head steps for all such u, v, or it reaches v for all of them. Without pairs one gets only that t u v reaches u or v, with the choice possibly depending on u and v. With pairs: (t, t) ∈ ⋂R (R ⇒ (R ⇒ R)). Take R = Gen({(u, u′), (v, v′)}). Then (t u v, t u′ v′) ∈ R, so by M2 (generated) for pairs the two terms reach, by head steps taken together, the pair (u, u′) or the pair (v, v′); a member of Base is excluded because both terms are closed. If t u v reaches u, then it does not reach v: a term has at most one head step, so if it reached both, one of u, v would reach the other. So the pair reached is (u, u′), and t u′ v′ reaches u′. So the choice is the same for every pair of inputs.

These are the simplest cases of what Reynolds and Wadler draw from parametricity. The second is the one that shows the gain: the relation R ties the behaviour of t on one pair of inputs to its behaviour on another, which no set of terms can do.

Example · Two pairs, and what the relation of ∀X.X→X is not

Worked out for this page by a reader of the paper of Plotkin and Abadi, and not reviewed. All four terms of the two pairs are typable with ∀X.X→X by the rules of {{rules}}, so the abstraction theorem speaks of each of them.

  • Related, and not the same term. The pair (λx. I (I x), λx. K x x) is in the relation that ∀X.X→X names. Take a relation R and (u, u′) ∈ R. The pair of the two terms applied to u and u′ takes three head steps, to (I (I u), K u′ u′), to (I u, (λy.u′) u′) and to (u, u′). On each side the arguments are u or u′ or I u, which are in SN, so the steps are safe, and the closure condition, three times, puts the pair in R.
  • One step apart, and not related. The term λx. I x takes one step, inside the abstraction, to I. The pair (λx. I x, I) is not in that relation. Take R = Gen({(I, I)}). The pair ((λx. I x) I, I I) takes one head step, to (I I, I). That pair takes no head step, because its right side has none; it is not (I, I); and it is not in Base. So by M2 (generated), read for pairs, ((λx. I x) I, I I) is not in R, although (I, I) is.

So the relation that this closed type expression names is not “the same term”, by the pair of the item related, and not the same term, whose two terms are also joined by steps, both reaching I, and it does not contain every pair of terms that are joined by steps, by the pair of the item one step apart, and not related. That pair fails for one reason only: a pair takes its head steps on both sides at once, and its two sides need different numbers of them, two on the left and one on the right.

Quoted from Plotkin and Abadi. Their logic for parametricity has a lemma called identity extension: the relation of a type expression, with equality put for its variables, is equality. Their equality contains the equations for β and for η. Worked out for this page, and not reviewed. With “the same term” for equality, the pair of the item related, and not the same term shows that the relation here is not inside equality; with “joined by steps” for equality, the pair of the item one step apart, and not related shows that equality is not inside the relation.

Definition · Equality relations, and two-sided relations

An equality relation is a relation that is symmetric and transitive: if (t, t′) is a member, then so is (t′, t); and if (t, t′) and (t′, t″) are members, then so is (t, t″).

For a relation R, dom(R) is the set of the terms t with (t, t) ∈ R.

A relation is two-sided if it is closed under undoing a safe head step on either side alone: if s →h s′ is safe and (s′, t) is a member, then (s, t) is a member; and the same on the right side.

Z is the set of the pairs (s, t) in SN such that the head steps of s lead to a term x u1…un and those of t to a term x v1…vn, with the same x and the same n.

The literature calls an equality relation a partial equivalence relation: it is an equivalence relation on dom(R) and says nothing of other terms. The name “two-sided” is this page’s; no name for it was looked for.

Example · Three relations

  • The relation of all pairs in SN is an equality relation, and it is two-sided, by R3 (safe head step) on one side.
  • Gen({(I, I)}) is an equality relation: by M2 (generated), read for pairs, its members are the pairs whose head steps, taken together, lead to (I, I) or to a member of Base, and it is symmetric and transitive by the argument that the lemma “Smaller collections of relations carry the theorem” gives for the smallest relation, with (I, I) as one more place where the head steps may end. It is not two-sided: it contains (I, I) and not (I I, I), by the example of two pairs.
  • Gen({(I, K)}) is not symmetric, so it is not an equality relation: (K, I) takes no head step, is not (I, K), and is not in Base.

Lemma · Smaller collections of relations carry the theoremhere, reviewed

equality
The smallest relation and the relation of all pairs in SN are equality relations. If R and S are equality relations, then so is R ⇒ S. The intersection of a nonempty set of equality relations is an equality relation.
two-sided
If R is a relation and S is two-sided, then R ⇒ S is two-sided. The intersection of a nonempty set of two-sided relations is two-sided. Z is the smallest two-sided relation; it is an equality relation, and it has no closed pair.
theorem
Let ∀ range over the equality relations, or over the two-sided relations, or over the relations that are both, and let assignments take their values in the same collection. Then every type expression names a relation of the collection, and Lemma N, the four facts, the soundness theorem and both corollaries hold, read for pairs, and so does the abstraction theorem.
members
For an equality relation R, dom(R) is a type set, and both sides of every member of R are in it.
extensional
Let R and S be equality relations, and let f ∈ dom(R ⇒ S). Then, for every term f′: (f, f′) ∈ R ⇒ S if and only if (f u, f′ u) ∈ S for every u ∈ dom(R).
one step apart
If ∀ ranges over the two-sided relations, the pair (λx. I x, I) is in the relation that ∀X.X→X names.

With ∀ over all relations, whether the relation of a closed type expression is transitive is open on this page. With ∀ over the equality relations it is symmetric and transitive by the item theorem, and the item extensional is function extensionality for it: a function that the relation relates to itself is related to another term exactly if the two agree, up to the relation, on every argument that is related to itself. The hypothesis f ∈ dom(R ⇒ S) is used, and without it the “if” half fails. With ∀ over the two-sided relations the number of head steps of each side no longer matters, and the pair of the item one step apart, and not related of the example of two pairs becomes related. Each smaller collection gives fewer relations to instantiate a ∀ at: the relation Gen({(u, u′), (v, v′)}) of the free theorem “Choice” is not an equality relation in general, and it is not two-sided, because it contains (u, u′) and not (I u, u′). The reader who reviewed this lemma derived that the free theorem “Choice” still holds over all three collections if u, v, u′, v′ are closed abstractions; that proof is in the review, has had no second reader, and is not given here.

Quoted from Ahmed’s report on relations indexed by a number of steps. She considers relations in which “both terms are required to terminate in exactly the same number of steps” and sets them aside: “such a logical relation would not be very useful (unless we are concerned with reasoning about timing leaks in an information-flow setting)”. Of an earlier model that was called a model of partial equivalence relations she writes that the name “may be somewhat of misnomer”, “since the status of transitivity is unclear”; her direct proof of transitivity is for a language with no ∀, and with ∀ and no ∃ she shows her relation to be equality in every context. Here: with ∀ over all relations, the relation that a type expression names is of the sort she sets aside, as the item one step apart, and not related shows; with ∀ over the two-sided relations it is not.

Quoted from Allen’s thesis of 1987, which gives a semantics to Martin-Löf’s type theory. Symmetry and transitivity are part of his definition: a type system has “a three-place relation t = s ∈ T” that “is transitive and symmetric in t and s”, with “t = s ∈ T iff ∃t′. t′←t & t′ = s ∈ T”, where t′←t says that t′ is the value of t. There only closed terms have values, and no quantifier ranges over all the type expressions. Here: so in his definition a term is seen only through its value, each side takes its steps alone, and a variable is a member of the relation of no type expression. Status. The item equality, with the item theorem for it, was found by the readers of these two sources for this page, each without knowing of the other; the items on two-sided relations by the reader of Allen’s thesis.

Proof

Equality. The smallest relation consists of the pairs in SN whose head steps, taken together, lead to a member of Base, by M2 (generated) read for pairs. It is symmetric, because Base is. Let (a, b) and (b, c) be members: the first reaches a pair of Base after k head steps of the pair, the second after m. So b reaches a variable applied to arguments after k head steps and after m; such a term has no head step, and a term has at most one head step, so k = m and the two pairs of Base have the same middle term. So (a, c) reaches, after k head steps of the pair, a pair with the same variable and the same number of arguments on both sides, which is in Base; and the steps are safe on both sides. The relation of all pairs in SN is symmetric and transitive at once.

Let R and S be equality relations; R ⇒ S is a relation by the arrow lemma read for pairs. Symmetric: let (t, t′) ∈ R ⇒ S and (u, u′) ∈ R. Then (u′, u) ∈ R, so (t u′, t′ u) ∈ S, so (t′ u, t u′) ∈ S. Transitive: let (t, t′) and (t′, t″) be in R ⇒ S and (u, u″) ∈ R. Then (u″, u) ∈ R, and so (u″, u″) ∈ R. So (t u, t′ u″) ∈ S and (t′ u″, t″ u″) ∈ S, and (t u, t″ u″) ∈ S. Both properties pass to intersections.

Two-sided. For the arrow: let s →h s′ be safe, (s′, t) ∈ R ⇒ S and (u, u′) ∈ R. Then (s′ u, t u′) ∈ S, and s u →h s′ u is safe, with the same argument, so (s u, t u′) ∈ S; the same on the right side. Intersections: at once. The set Z contains Base. It is two-sided: undoing a safe head step on one side keeps that side in SN, by R3 (safe head step), and does not change where its head steps lead. A two-sided set of pairs is closed under undoing a safe head step of the pair, one side after the other, so Z is a relation. Every two-sided relation contains Base and is closed under undoing a safe head step on one side, so it contains Z. Z is symmetric, and transitive because a term has one sequence of head steps. A side of a member has the variable x free, so no member is closed.

Theorem. By the remark “What the proof asked of the collection that ∀ ranges over” in {{rules}}, read for pairs. Each of the three collections consists of relations; it is closed under ⇒ and under intersections of nonempty sets of members, by the items equality and two-sided; and it is not empty, because the relation of all pairs in SN is in all three. Its smallest member has no closed pair: for the equality relations it is the smallest relation; for the other two collections it is Z.

Members. dom(R) is inside SN. It contains Base, because (b, b) is in Base for pairs if b is in Base. If s →h s′ is safe and (s′, s′) ∈ R, then (s, s) ∈ R, by undoing the head step of the pair. If (t, t′) ∈ R, then (t′, t) ∈ R, and so (t, t) and (t′, t′) are in R.

Extensional. If (f, f′) ∈ R ⇒ S and u ∈ dom(R), then (u, u) ∈ R, so (f u, f′ u) ∈ S. In the other direction, let (u, u′) ∈ R. Then (f u, f u′) ∈ S, because (f, f) ∈ R ⇒ S; and u′ ∈ dom(R), so (f u′, f′ u′) ∈ S; so (f u, f′ u′) ∈ S. For the note: let R = Gen({(I, I), (I, K), (K, I), (K, K)}) and S = Gen({(I, I), (K, K)}), which are equality relations, and f = f′ = I. Then (I w, I w) ∈ S for every w ∈ dom(R), and (I I, I K) reaches (I, K), which is not in S; so (I, I) is not in R ⇒ S.

One step apart. Let R be two-sided and (u, u′) ∈ R. The term I u′ takes a safe head step to u′, so (u, I u′) ∈ R. The term (λx. I x) u takes two safe head steps, to I u and to u, so ((λx. I x) u, I u′) ∈ R. So the pair is in R ⇒ R for every two-sided R. ∎

Remark · What changes, and what does not

Everything built on the main line by the same words runs on pairs too: the additions of {{and}} and {{more}}, the families of {{fam}}, which become functions from pairs to relations, and the three collections of {{families}}, where a head-closed relation is determined, by the same correspondence, by its pairs with no joint head step; among those are the pairs of values, and restricted to them it is a relation between values, which is the form in which parametricity is usually stated, but mixed pairs such as (I, y) are among them too. This was checked for this page by reading the proofs and is not written out.

What changes is what equality can mean. Under the relational reading, two functions are related at R ⇒ S exactly if they send related arguments to related results, so an equality read as a relation is extensional from the start, and function extensionality, quotient types and observational equality, which {{equalities}} had to turn away, have their home here. The lemma “Smaller collections of relations carry the theorem” takes the first step, for function extensionality; quotient types and observational equality are not worked out on this page. Homotopy type theory and cubical type theory, where a proof of an equality carries structure of its own, are further away still, and this page names no home for them. The lemma “Type sets tell terms apart” of {{structure}} still holds for relations, read for pairs; what the relational reading adds is not that the semantics identifies more terms, but that a type expression can now relate two different terms.
