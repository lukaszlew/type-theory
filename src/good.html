Very little of the proof is about strong normalization. It uses three facts about the set SN, and they follow from Lemma R. Applied to a variable: if t x is in the set, then so is t. Variable applied: if u1,…,un are in the set, then so is x u1…un. Safe head step: if s →h s′, and s′ and the argument of the step are in the set, then so is s.

Replace SN everywhere by any set Good of terms with these three properties, and “strongly normalizing” by “in Good”. Every definition and proof that the theorem and its corollaries use goes through unchanged: the definition of a type set, the arrow lemma, the intersection lemma, the four facts, Lemma N, the theorem and both corollaries. The conclusion is that every typable term is in Good. Three things use more about SN, namely that every subterm of a member of SN is in SN: the membership condition in M2 (generated), the forms E2 (one argument at a time) and E3 (whole term in SN) of Lemma E, and Lemma L. Strong normalization is the case Good = SN, and it enters only through Lemma R. This generalization goes back to Mitchell, and Gallier’s survey works it out. It is the same move as in the remark of {{rules}} on what the proof asked of the collection that ∀ ranges over, one level down: there the collection of sets was replaced, here the largest set.

The weakly normalizing terms are another set with the three properties. Variable applied holds because a run of x u1…un runs the arguments one at a time, and safe head step because a normal form of s′ is reached from s through its head step. Applied to a variable needs a sentence: if t x has a normal form, then so does t. By the standardization theorem, quoted here, a term with a normal form reaches it by the run that always takes the leftmost step. The leftmost run of t x takes the leftmost steps of t until t has become an abstraction λy.t′, if it ever does; if it never does, that run is the leftmost run of t with x appended, so t has a normal form. If it does, the run continues with (λy.t′) x → t′[x/y], and the leftmost run of t′[x/y] ends in a normal form. The same steps can be taken in t′, because replacing a variable by a variable neither creates nor removes a redex, and they end in a term m with m[x/y] equal to that normal form; a redex in m would be a redex in m[x/y], so m is a normal form too. So λy.t′, and with it t, has a normal form. This argument was worked out for this page.

Lemma · Weak normalization with no theorem quotedhere, reviewed

Let WN be the smallest set of terms that is closed under the three rules of the generation lemma of {{gen}}, with the rule head step read without its condition on the argument: if s →h s′ and s′ ∈ WN, then s ∈ WN.

normal forms
Every member of WN has a normal form.
good
WN has the three properties: applied to a variable, variable applied and safe head step.

So the proof of this section, run with this set as Good, shows that every typable term has a normal form, and the standardization theorem is not used. That theorem is needed for one thing only, which the conclusion does not need: that every term with a normal form is in WN. The definition of WN by these rules is Matthes’s, and so is the remark on where standardization is needed; the proof of applied to a variable was written by a reader of his thesis for this page and checked by the writer.

Proof

Normal forms. By induction on the rules. A variable applied to terms with normal forms has one; so does an abstraction of a term that has one; and a term that takes a head step to a term with a normal form reaches that normal form.

Good. Variable applied and safe head step are two of the rules. For applied to a variable, note first that a term enters WN by exactly one rule, the one for its form, and from premises that the term determines: the three forms of {{head}} exclude each other, and a term has only one head step.

Renaming. If r[x/y] ∈ WN for variables x and y, then r ∈ WN. By induction on how r[x/y] entered WN; it has the form of r. If r is a variable applied to r1,…,rn, then each ri[x/y] entered before, so each ri is in WN, and so is r. If r is λz.r′, with z chosen different from x and y, the same with r′. If r is (λz.r′) s r1…rn, then the term after the head step of r[x/y] is (r′[s/z] r1…rn)[x/y], which entered before; so r′[s/z] r1…rn ∈ WN, and r ∈ WN by the rule head step.

Applied to a variable. Let t x ∈ WN; by induction on how it entered. If t is a variable applied to u1,…,un, the rule gives each ui ∈ WN, so t ∈ WN. If t is (λz.r) s u1…un, then the term after the head step of t x is (r[s/z] u1…un) x, which entered before; by induction r[s/z] u1…un ∈ WN, so t ∈ WN by the rule head step. If t is λz.r, with z chosen different from x, then the term after the head step of t x is r[x/z], which is in WN; so r ∈ WN by renaming, and t ∈ WN by the rule abstraction. ∎

With Good = SN no type set satisfies T = T ⇒ T, as limit 2 in {{limits}} shows. With Good = all terms, the set of all terms does.

So SN is the smallest set with the three properties. The reason is the generation lemma of {{gen}}: every Good is closed under its three rules. Variable applied is the property of that name, and head step is safe head step. For abstraction, applied to a variable reduces the claim about λx.t to (λx.t) x, and safe head step reduces that to t, since the argument x is in the set by variable applied.
